Maxwell's Equations from the U(1) Gauge Action

Standard electromagnetism, derived from a gauge-invariant Lagrangian by variation

This page derives the four Maxwell equations from the U(1) Yang-Mills action in the abelian (linear) limit. This is textbook gauge field theory — no additional physical assumptions are used beyond gauge invariance and the standard variational principle.

1. The Gauge Field and Field Strength

Introduce a U(1) gauge connection $A_\mu$ (the four-potential). Its field strength (curvature) is the antisymmetric tensor

\[F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu\]

which is invariant under the gauge transformation $A_\mu \to A_\mu + \partial_\mu\lambda$ for any scalar function $\lambda(x)$.

2. The Action

The gauge-invariant, Lorentz-invariant action built from $F_{\mu\nu}$ at lowest order, with a matter current $J^\mu$ minimally coupled, is

\[S = \int d^4x \left[-\tfrac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} - J^\mu A_\mu\right]\]

3. Equation of Motion (Inhomogeneous Maxwell Equations)

Varying $S$ with respect to $A_\mu$ and applying the Euler–Lagrange equation $\partial_\nu\!\left(\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\nu A_\mu)}\right) - \dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial A_\mu} = 0$ gives

\[\partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu} = J^\mu\]

This single tensor equation contains two of Maxwell's four equations. Writing out the $\mu=0$ and $\mu=i$ components in terms of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ (via $F^{0i}=E^i$, $F^{ij}=-\epsilon^{ijk}B_k$) recovers

\[\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho \qquad\qquad \nabla\times\vec B - \partial_t\vec E = \vec J\]

(Gauss's law and the Ampère–Maxwell law, in units with $\varepsilon_0=\mu_0=1$.)

4. Bianchi Identity (Homogeneous Maxwell Equations)

Because $F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu$ is built from a potential, it automatically satisfies the Bianchi identity

\[\partial_{[\lambda}F_{\mu\nu]} = 0\]

which is equivalent to the remaining two Maxwell equations:

\[\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0 \qquad\qquad \nabla\times\vec E + \partial_t\vec B = 0\]

(No magnetic monopoles, and Faraday's law.) These hold identically — they are a consequence of $F$ having a potential, not a separate dynamical equation.

5. Charge Conservation

Because $F^{\mu\nu}$ is antisymmetric, $\partial_\mu\partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu} \equiv 0$, so the equation of motion $\partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu}=J^\mu$ forces

\[\partial_\mu J^\mu = 0\]

Charge conservation is automatic once the gauge-invariant action is written down; it need not be imposed separately.

Summary

All four of Maxwell's equations follow from two ingredients: (1) an action built from the field-strength tensor of a U(1) connection, varied by the standard Euler–Lagrange procedure, and (2) the Bianchi identity, which is automatic once $F_{\mu\nu}$ is derived from a potential $A_\mu$. No extra structure, free parameters, or additional physical postulates are required beyond gauge invariance itself.

Standard gauge field theory (see e.g. Jackson, Classical Electrodynamics; Peskin & Schroeder, An Introduction to Quantum Field Theory, ch. 2 and 15).
