Gauge Emergence: Rigorous Derivation

From Orbifold Geometry to Yang-Mills Without Circular Logic

Addresses: Gauge emergence circularity gap

The Problem and Its Resolution

The Circularity Problem

Previous derivations assumed "observables depend only on closed-loop holonomies" to derive gauge symmetry. This is circular: gauge symmetry means observables are holonomy-dependent.

The Resolution

Gauge symmetry emerges from a purely geometric fact: the Z₃ helix S1/Z2 has a non-contractible cycle. On such spaces, parallel transport is path-dependent, and the path integral automatically sums over topological sectors classified by holonomies. No assumption about observables is needed.

1. The Geometric Origin of Gauge Symmetry

1.1 Non-Contractible Cycles Force Holonomy Sectors

The Z₃ helix M4 × S1/Z2 has fundamental group:

\[\boxed{\pi_1(S^1/\mathbb{Z}_2) = \mathbb{Z}}\]
(1.1) — Non-trivial fundamental group

Any path that winds around the S1 factor cannot be continuously shrunk to a point. This is a topological fact, not an assumption.

1.2 Parallel Transport on Non-Contractible Spaces

Consider any field ψ(x,X) on the Z₃ helix. Parallel transport around the non-contractible cycle gives:

\[\psi(x, X + 2L_X) = U[\gamma] \cdot \psi(x, X)\]
(1.2)

where U[γ] is the holonomy matrix. For the theory to be well-defined:

\[\boxed{U[\gamma]^n = 1 \quad \text{for some } n \in \mathbb{Z}}\]
(1.3) — Consistency condition (forces compact group)

This forces the structure group to be compact (U(N), SU(N), SO(N), Sp(N), or exceptional groups).

1.3 The Key Theorem

Theorem 1.1 — Gauge Symmetry from Geometry

On any space with π1 ≠ 0, the path integral naturally decomposes into topological sectors labeled by holonomies. The sum over sectors is equivalent to integrating over a gauge field.

Proof Sketch:

1. The path integral sums over all field configurations:

\[Z = \int \mathcal{D}\phi\, e^{iS[\phi]}\]

2. Configurations on M4 × S1/Z2 are classified by their behavior under transport around S1:

\[\mathcal{D}\phi = \sum_{[U] \in \pi_1} \mathcal{D}\phi_{[U]}\]

3. The holonomy U is parametrized as U = exp(i∮A), introducing a connection A:

\[Z = \int \mathcal{D}A\, \sum_{[U]} \int \mathcal{D}\phi_{[U]}\, e^{iS[\phi, A]}\]

4. Summing over sectors is equivalent to integrating over the gauge field A with the constraint that A is a connection (transforms as a gauge field).

\[\boxed{\text{Non-trivial } \pi_1 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Gauge field in path integral}}\]
(1.4) — Gauge emergence theorem

2. Why Yang-Mills Structure?

2.1 The Connection Must Have Dynamics

The gauge field A introduced by the topological decomposition must have its own dynamics. What is the most general action for A consistent with:

  • 5D diffeomorphism invariance
  • Gauge invariance (under A → gAg-1 + g∂g-1)
  • At most two derivatives (renormalizability)

2.2 Uniqueness of Yang-Mills Action

Theorem 2.1 — Yang-Mills Is Unique

The unique action satisfying (1)-(3) is the Yang-Mills action:

\[\boxed{S_{\rm YM} = -\frac{1}{4g_5^2} \int d^5x\, \sqrt{-g}\, \text{Tr}(F_{MN} F^{MN})}\]

This is analogous to the Lovelock theorem for gravity: symmetry + locality + renormalizability = unique action.

Proof:

Gauge-invariant quantities built from A with dimension ≤ 5:

  • FMNFMN — dimension 4 ✓
  • εMNPQRTr(FMNFPQAR) — dimension 5, but is a total derivative
  • Tr(A2) — not gauge invariant ✗

The Yang-Mills term is the unique gauge-invariant kinetic term.
