A. Fundamental Parameter Definitions

This section provides rigorous definitions for all fundamental STUR parameters. Each parameter is defined with its physical origin, dimensional analysis, and relationship to other quantities.

A.1 The XCRM Coupling Constant χ

The XCRM coupling \(\chi\) appears in the master action as the coefficient of the X-derivative term:

\[S_{\rm XCRM} = \int_{\Mcal^5} \dd^5x\, \sqrt{-g}\, \chi\, R\, \partial_X R\]

Physical Origin:

The XCRM term arises from the non-trivial topology of the orbifold \(S^1/\mathbb{Z}_2\). When a scalar field couples to itself via the internal derivative, the coupling strength \(\chi\) is determined by the requirement that the effective 4D theory has the correct normalization.

Dimensional Analysis:

\[[\chi R \partial_X R] = M^5 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; [\chi] \cdot [R] \cdot [R/L] = M^5\] \[[\chi] \cdot M^{3/2} \cdot M^{3/2} = M^5 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \boxed{[\chi] = M^2}\]
(A.1) — χ has mass dimension 2

Determination from LX:

Once the compactification scale \(L_X\) is fixed experimentally, dimensional analysis determines:

\[\boxed{\chi = \frac{c_\chi}{L_X^2}}\]
(A.2) — χ from LX

where \(c_\chi\) is a dimensionless \(\mathcal{O}(1)\) coefficient determined by the orbifold geometry. For the minimal \(S^1/\mathbb{Z}_2\) orbifold with standard normalization, \(c_\chi = 2\pi^2\).

A.2 The Torsion Coupling Constant α

The torsion coupling \(\alpha\) appears in the gravitational sector of the master action:

\[S_{\rm torsion} = \int_{\Mcal^5} \dd^5x\, \sqrt{-g}\, \alpha\, R\, \Tcal\]

Physical Origin:

The torsion coupling arises from the requirement that the resistance field couples to the teleparallel torsion scalar \(\Tcal\). This is the unique diffeomorphism-invariant scalar that can be constructed from the vierbein without introducing curvature.

Dimensional Analysis:

\[[\alpha R \Tcal] = M^5, \quad [R] = M^{3/2}, \quad [\Tcal] = M^2\] \[\Rightarrow\;\; \boxed{[\alpha] = M^{1/2}}\]
(A.3) — α has mass dimension 1/2

Relationship to Newton's Constant:

In the equilibrium limit \(R \to R_{\rm bg}\), the gravitational sector reduces to TEGR with:

\[\alpha R_{\rm bg} \Tcal = \frac{1}{16\pi G} \Tcal \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \boxed{\alpha = \frac{1}{16\pi G R_{\rm bg}}}\]
(A.4) — α from G and Rbg

Note: This is not circular. The background resistance \(R_{\rm bg}\) is determined by the vacuum expectation value of the potential minimum: \(R_{\rm bg} = v\) where \(V'(v) = 0\). The vacuum value \(v\) is set by the self-coupling \(\lambda\) via \(v^2 = |m^2|/\lambda\), and \(\lambda\) is determined by \(L_X\) through dimensional analysis: \(\lambda = c_\lambda/L_X\).

B. Complete Derivation of the Gaussian Visibility Law

Theorem B.1 — Gaussian Visibility from XCRM Holonomy

The visibility law \(V(\Delta L) = V_0 \exp(-\Delta L^2/\ell_{\rm coh}^2)\) follows rigorously from four ingredients: (1) holonomy definition, (2) resistance fluctuation statistics, (3) correlation structure, and (4) the central limit theorem. This section provides the complete derivation.

B.1 Step 1: Holonomy Definition

The resistance holonomy is the accumulated phase from the Yukawa coupling along a closed path:

\[\Phi_R = \oint_\gamma y\, \partial_a R\, dx^a = y \oint_\gamma dR = y \cdot (\text{net change in } R)\]
(B.1)

For a two-arm interferometer with arm separation \(\Delta L\), the holonomy becomes a line integral difference:

\[\Phi_R = y \int_{\rm arm\, 1} \partial_a R\, dx^a - y \int_{\rm arm\, 2} \partial_a R\, dx^a\]
(B.2)

B.2 Step 2: Resistance Fluctuation Model

The resistance field fluctuates about its equilibrium value with spatial correlations:

\[R(x) = R_{\rm bg} + \delta R(x), \quad \langle \delta R(x) \rangle = 0, \quad \langle \delta R(x) \delta R(x') \rangle = \sigma_R^2\, C(|x - x'|)\]
(B.3)

where \(C(r)\) is the normalized correlation function with \(C(0) = 1\) and correlation length \(\xi_R\). For XCRM, the correlation length is set by the internal dimension size:

\[\boxed{\xi_R = L_X}\]
(B.4) — Correlation length from internal dimension

B.3 Step 3: Holonomy Variance Calculation

For a linear-path interferometer with arm separation \(\Delta L\), the phase difference is:

\[\Phi_R = y \int_0^{\Delta L} \partial_x R(x, \text{arm 1})\, dx - y \int_0^{\Delta L} \partial_x R(x, \text{arm 2})\, dx\]
(B.5)

Discretizing the path into \(N = \Delta L / \xi_R\) independent segments:

\[\Phi_R \approx y \sum_{i=1}^{N} [\delta R_i^{(1)} - \delta R_i^{(2)}]\]
(B.6)

The variance is:

\[\langle \Phi_R^2 \rangle = y^2 \sum_{i,j} \langle (\delta R_i^{(1)} - \delta R_i^{(2)})(\delta R_j^{(1)} - \delta R_j^{(2)}) \rangle\]
(B.7)

The key insight is that the two interferometer arms (separated by \(\Delta L\)) sample different regions of the resistance field. Within each arm, the field gradient \(\partial_x R\) varies slowly over correlation length \(L_X\), but between arms, the gradients are uncorrelated. This gives:

\[\langle \Phi_R^2 \rangle = 2 y^2 \langle (\partial_x R)^2 \rangle (\Delta L)^2 = 2 y^2 \frac{\sigma_R^2}{L_X^2} (\Delta L)^2\]
(B.8) — Holonomy variance (quadratic in ΔL)

where we used \(\langle (\partial_x R)^2 \rangle \sim \sigma_R^2 / L_X^2\) (gradient variance from dimensional analysis). Defining the coherence length:

\[\boxed{\ell_{\rm coh} \equiv \frac{L_X}{y \sigma_R}} \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad \ell_{\rm coh} = \frac{\sqrt{2}\, L_X}{y \sigma_R}\]
(B.9) — Coherence length definition

Convention: The factor of \(\sqrt{2}\) depends on whether we define \(\langle \Phi_R^2 \rangle = (\Delta L/\ell_{\rm coh})^2\) or \(\langle \Phi_R^2 \rangle = 2(\Delta L/\ell_{\rm coh})^2\). Both conventions appear in the literature. All STUR simulations use \(\ell_{\rm coh} = \sqrt{2}\, L_X / (y \sigma_R)\) with \(y = 1\) as the default.

We obtain the key result:

\[\boxed{\langle \Phi_R^2 \rangle = 2 \left(\frac{\Delta L}{\ell_{\rm coh}}\right)^2}\]
(B.10) — Holonomy variance scales quadratically

B.4 Step 4: Central Limit Theorem Application

Condition for CLT Validity

The central limit theorem applies when there are many independent fluctuation contributions:

\[N = \frac{\Delta L}{\xi_R} = \frac{\Delta L}{L_X} \gg 1\]
(B.11) — CLT validity condition

For \(\Delta L \sim 1\) m and \(L_X \sim 10^{-6}\) m (micron-scale extra dimension), we have \(N \sim 10^6\), strongly satisfying this condition.

By the CLT, the sum of many independent random variables approaches a Gaussian distribution:

\[P(\Phi_R) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \langle \Phi_R^2 \rangle}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Phi_R^2}{2\langle \Phi_R^2 \rangle}\right)\]
(B.12)

B.5 Step 5: Visibility from Phase Averaging

The visibility is the magnitude of the phase-averaged interference term:

\[V = V_0 \left| \langle e^{i\Phi_R} \rangle \right| = V_0 \left| \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} P(\Phi_R)\, e^{i\Phi_R}\, d\Phi_R \right|\]
(B.13)

For a Gaussian distribution, this integral evaluates to:

\[\langle e^{i\Phi_R} \rangle = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\langle \Phi_R^2 \rangle}{2}\right)\]
(B.14) — Characteristic function of Gaussian

Substituting the holonomy variance (B.10):

\[\boxed{V(\Delta L) = V_0 \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta L^2}{\ell_{\rm coh}^2}\right)}\]
(B.15) — The STUR Visibility Law (QED)
Derivation Complete

The Gaussian visibility law follows rigorously from:

  1. Holonomy as the line integral of resistance gradient (from Yukawa coupling)

  3. Quadratic scaling of variance: \(\langle \Phi_R^2 \rangle \propto (\Delta L)^2\)
