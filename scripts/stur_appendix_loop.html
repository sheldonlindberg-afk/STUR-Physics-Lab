C.1 Why Loop Geometry Matters

Key Insight: Area vs. Path

Linear interferometers measure visibility as a function of path difference \(\Matt{\Delta L}\). Loop interferometers (Sagnac, ring) measure visibility as a function of enclosed area \(\Matt{A}\). For STUR, loops can provide orders of magnitude enhancement over linear geometries.

This appendix provides a complete derivation of the area-scaling law for closed-loop interferometers, starting from Stokes' theorem applied to the XCRM holonomy.

C.2 XCRM Holonomy: Line vs. Surface Integral

Definition

The XCRM holonomy is defined as a line integral of the resistance current around a closed path:

\[\boxed{\Quant{\Phi_R} = \oint_{\partial\Sigma} \XCRM{\mathsf{J}_a}\, dx^a}\]
(C.1) — Holonomy as line integral

where \(\XCRM{\mathsf{J}_a} = y\,\partial_a R\) is the phase-gradient 1-form induced by the Yukawa coupling.

Stokes' Theorem

By Stokes' theorem, this line integral can be converted to a surface integral over any surface \(\Sigma\) bounded by the loop:

\[\boxed{\Quant{\Phi_R} = \oint_{\partial\Sigma} \XCRM{\mathsf{J}_a}\, dx^a = \int_\Sigma (\nabla \times \XCRM{\mathsf{J}}) \cdot d\mathbf{A} = \int_\Sigma \XCRM{\mathsf{F}_{ab}}\, dA^{ab}}\]
(C.2) — Stokes' theorem application

where \(\XCRM{\mathsf{F}_{ab}} = \partial_a \mathsf{J}_b - \partial_b \mathsf{J}_a = y(\partial_a \partial_b R - \partial_b \partial_a R)\) is the curvature of the resistance 1-form.

Key Point: Non-Zero Curvature

For a smooth \(\Diff{R(x)}\) field, the curvature \(\XCRM{\mathsf{F}_{ab}}\) would vanish identically. However, stochastic fluctuations in \(\Diff{R}\) generate non-zero effective curvature when averaged over the XCRM bath. This is analogous to how Brownian motion generates effective diffusion even though individual particle paths are deterministic.

C.3 Holonomy Variance for Loop Geometry

Setup

Consider a planar loop of area \(\Matt{A_{\rm loop}}\) in the \((x,y)\) plane. The holonomy is:

\[\Quant{\Phi_R} = \int_\Sigma \XCRM{\mathsf{F}_{xy}}\, dA = \int_\Sigma y (\partial_x \partial_y - \partial_y \partial_x)\Diff{R}\, dA\]
(C.3) — Holonomy for planar loop

Fluctuation Model

The resistance fluctuations have the correlation structure:

\[\langle \Diff{\delta R}(\mathbf{x}) \Diff{\delta R}(\mathbf{x'}) \rangle = \Diff{\sigma_R}^2\, C(|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x'}|/\XCRM{L_X})\]
(C.4) — Two-point correlation

where \(C(u)\) is a dimensionless correlation function with \(C(0) = 1\) and \(C(u) \to 0\) for \(u \gg 1\).

Variance Calculation

The holonomy variance involves a four-point integral:

\[\langle \Quant{\Phi_R}^2 \rangle = y^2 \int_\Sigma \int_\Sigma \langle \XCRM{\mathsf{F}_{xy}}(\mathbf{x}) \XCRM{\mathsf{F}_{xy}}(\mathbf{x'}) \rangle\, dA\, dA'\]
(C.5) — Variance integral

The curvature-curvature correlator involves fourth derivatives of the R-correlator. For a Gaussian random field:

\[\langle \XCRM{\mathsf{F}_{xy}}(\mathbf{x}) \XCRM{\mathsf{F}_{xy}}(\mathbf{x'}) \rangle \sim \frac{y^2 \Diff{\sigma_R}^2}{\XCRM{L_X}^4}\, \delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x'})\]
(C.6) — Curvature correlator (white-noise limit)

where the delta function arises because fluctuations at separations \(\gg \XCRM{L_X}\) are uncorrelated. Substituting:

\[\boxed{\langle \Quant{\Phi_R}^2 \rangle_{\rm loop} = \frac{y^2 \Diff{\sigma_R}^2}{\XCRM{L_X}^4} \cdot \Matt{A_{\rm loop}}}\]
(C.7) — Holonomy variance for loops (area scaling)

C.4 Loop Visibility Law

Following the same Gaussian averaging as for linear interferometers (see Predictions):

\[\boxed{\Matt{V_{\rm loop}} = V_0 \exp\!\left(-\frac{\langle \Quant{\Phi_R}^2 \rangle_{\rm loop}}{2}\right) = V_0 \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Matt{A_{\rm loop}}^2}{\Matt{A_{\rm coh}}^2}\right)}\]
(C.8) — Loop visibility law

where the coherence area is:

\[\boxed{\Matt{A_{\rm coh}} = \frac{\XCRM{L_X}^2}{y \Diff{\sigma_R}} = \Matt{\ell_{\rm coh}} \cdot \XCRM{L_X}}\]
(C.9) — Coherence area definition
Key Result: Quadratic in Area

Loop visibility decays as \(\exp(-A^2/A_{\rm coh}^2)\) — quadratic in enclosed area, not linear in perimeter. This is the direct analog of the linear-path result \(\exp(-\Delta L^2/\ell_{\rm coh}^2)\).

C.5 Loop Enhancement Factor

Comparison: Loop vs. Linear

Consider a square loop of side \(L\) (perimeter \(4L\), area \(L^2\)) and a linear interferometer with path difference \(\Matt{\Delta L} = L\). The holonomy variances are:

Geometry\(\langle \Phi_R^2 \rangle\)Scaling
Linear (path diff. \(\Delta L\)) \(\dfrac{y^2 \sigma_R^2}{L_X^2} \cdot \Delta L^2\) \(\propto \Delta L^2\)
Loop (area \(A = L^2\)) \(\dfrac{y^2 \sigma_R^2}{L_X^4} \cdot A\) \(\propto A = L^2\)

Enhancement Ratio

\[\boxed{\frac{\langle \Phi_R^2 \rangle_{\rm loop}}{\langle \Phi_R^2 \rangle_{\rm linear}} = \frac{A/L_X^4}{\Delta L^2/L_X^2} = \frac{A}{L_X^2 \cdot \Delta L^2}}\]
(C.10) — Loop enhancement ratio

Numerical Example

For a 1 m × 1 m square loop (\(A = 1\) m², \(\Delta L_{\rm eff} = 1\) m for the linear equivalent) with \(\XCRM{L_X} = 1\) μm:

\[\text{Enhancement} = \frac{1 \text{ m}^2}{(10^{-6} \text{ m})^2 \cdot (1 \text{ m})^2} = \frac{1}{10^{-12}} = 10^{12}\]
(C.11) — Example: trillion-fold enhancement
Implication: Macroscopic Observability

Even with microscopic \(\XCRM{L_X} \sim 1\) μm, loop geometries can amplify the STUR signal by factors of \(10^{12}\) or more. This provides a pathway to experimental detection even if the linear-path coherence length is astronomically large.

C.6 Optimal Loop Design

Maximizing Signal

For a fixed perimeter \(P\), the enclosed area is maximized by a circular loop:

\[A_{\rm circle} = \frac{P^2}{4\pi} \quad \text{vs.} \quad A_{\rm square} = \frac{P^2}{16}\]
(C.12) — Circle vs. square

A circle provides \(\pi/4 \approx 1.27\times\) more area than a square of the same perimeter.

Multi-Pass Enhancement

For \(N\) passes around the same loop (e.g., using mirrors), the effective area scales as:

\[\boxed{\Matt{A_{\rm eff}} = N^2 \cdot \Matt{A_{\rm single}}}\]
(C.13) — Multi-pass enhancement

The \(N^2\) scaling (not \(N\)) arises because phase accumulates coherently over multiple passes.

C.7 Sagnac Interferometer Specifics

Sagnac Advantage: Zero Path Difference

In a Sagnac interferometer, counter-propagating beams traverse the same path in opposite directions. The geometric path difference is \(\Matt{\Delta L} = 0\), eliminating standard decoherence mechanisms. Any visibility decay must arise from area-dependent effects like STUR.

Sagnac Holonomy

For a Sagnac loop of area \(\Matt{A}\), the XCRM holonomy difference between CW and CCW paths is:

\[\Quant{\Delta\Phi_R^{\rm Sagnac}} = \Quant{\Phi_R^{\rm CW}} - \Quant{\Phi_R^{\rm CCW}} = 2 \int_\Sigma \XCRM{\mathsf{F}_{xy}}\, dA\]
(C.14) — Sagnac holonomy difference

The factor of 2 arises because CW and CCW traverse the boundary in opposite senses, picking up opposite signs of the holonomy.

Sagnac Visibility

\[\boxed{\Matt{V_{\rm Sagnac}} = V_0 \exp\!\left(-2 \cdot \frac{\Matt{A}^2}{\Matt{A_{\rm coh}}^2}\right)}\]
(C.15) — Sagnac visibility (factor of 2 enhancement)

The extra factor of 2 in the exponent represents a \(\sqrt{2}\) enhancement in sensitivity compared to a single-pass loop.
