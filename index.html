Gravity, from the quantum level up.

Most people have heard that gravity is "curved space". Fewer know that Einstein's theory can be told another way, as a twist on perfectly flat space. Told that way, gravity looks like any other field in quantum physics, and you can ask a concrete question: what does gravity do to something that is in two places at once?

1 · Gravity as a twist

The same gravity as Einstein's, told a different way

In one line: space stays flat, every point carries its own rulers and clock, and gravity is how those rulers are twisted relative to their neighbours.

The famous picture. In 1915 Einstein described gravity as the shape of spacetime. A mass bends it, and everything moving nearby follows the bends. That's the rubber-sheet picture most of us grew up with.

The other picture. Einstein himself explored a second way in 1928, and physicists later showed it reproduces his theory exactly. It's called teleparallel gravity, or TEGR for short. In TEGR, space is never bent. Instead, every point in space carries its own little set of measuring sticks and a clock. Far from any mass, all those sets line up perfectly with one another. Near a mass, neighbouring sets stop lining up. That mismatch is called torsion, and it's what we feel as gravity.

Torsion behaves like a force field, much like the electric field around a charged object. It lives on a fixed, flat stage, and that is exactly the kind of field quantum physics already knows how to handle.

Same answers as Einstein, to every decimal we can measure

We solved gravity's equations in the twist form for the Sun and the Earth, then traced planets, light rays and clocks through the result. Curved space is used nowhere in that calculation.

A detail people find surprising: Newton's gravity predicts only half the bending of starlight. In the twist picture you can see where the other half comes from. The "time" rulers give Newton's half, and the "space" rulers give the rest, which Newton's theory has no place for.

Why tell gravity this way?

It's a field, like the others

Electricity, magnetism and the nuclear forces are all fields on a fixed stage. In TEGR, gravity becomes one more of them.

It fits quantum physics

Quantum physics asks what every field does when matter is in a superposition. With gravity as a field, the same question applies to it: section 2.

Nothing is given up

Black holes, orbits, light bending and GPS all come out exactly as in Einstein's curved picture. It's a different view of the same physics, not a rival theory.

2 · Gravity at the quantum level

Where is the gravity of something in two places at once?

In one line: each version of the object carries its own gravity field, just as it carries its own electric field.

Quantum objects can be in a superposition: not here or there, but a combination of both, which physicists call two "branches". Atoms and large molecules have been put in superpositions in the lab.

So what does their gravity do? There are two possibilities:

A. Gravity follows each branch

Each version of the object has its own gravity field. Two such objects can then become entangled through gravity alone, linked in a way no ordinary force-at-a-distance could manage.

This framework's answer, and also the answer of mainstream quantum gravity.

B. Gravity is an average

There is only one gravity field, pulling toward the object's "average" position. Then gravity can never entangle anything.

The answer of "classical gravity" models, such as Oppenheim's.

Why gravity can't simply be an average

An averaged gravity still has to respond to quantum matter, so it has to keep "checking" where the matter is. That checking makes it jitter, and the jitter shakes every atom it touches. Here's how big that shaking gets:

  • Smear the jitter at the scale where this framework counts its building blocks (section 3), and it would heat every kilogram of matter by about 0.5 W.
  • That's 6×10¹⁰ times the heat flowing out of the Earth's own interior. Nothing like it is observed.
  • So gravity follows each branch, like sound in a crystal follows each atom, rather than being an average like temperature.

And gravity doesn't collapse superpositions by itself. Splitting a tiny diamond into two places would send out about 10−59 gravitons, so gravity keeps essentially no record of which branch happened. A single diamond should stay fully in its superposition, as far as gravity is concerned.

3 · Where gravity's strength comes from

Empty space isn't empty, and that sets gravity's strength

In one line: empty space is quantum-entangled across every surface, and the amount of that entanglement per area sets how strong gravity is.

Quantum fields fill all of space, even a perfect vacuum. Draw any imaginary surface: the field on one side is entangled with the field on the other, by an amount that grows with the surface's area. We computed that amount directly.

In 1995 physicist Ted Jacobson showed something remarkable. Demand that ordinary heat-flow rules (thermodynamics) hold at every such surface, and Einstein's equations come out. Gravity's strength, Newton's constant G, is then fixed by how much entanglement there is per unit area. We rederived each step of his argument in code.

In this picture, gravity isn't added on top of quantum physics. It is what the quantum entanglement of empty space looks like on large scales.

open This explains where G comes from, but it doesn't predict G's value. Turned around, the measured G tells us how finely nature "counts" space: about a third of a Planck length per quantum field.

Go deeper: area law, Unruh, Jacobson cs = 0.3046, G = πa²c³/(N csħ)

checked here Area law by Srednicki's method: S = 0.3046 (R/a)² per scalar field (literature 0.30). Modular Hamiltonian on a lattice: K = 2π × boost to 0.99997 at L = 60 (Bisognano–Wichmann), so an accelerated observer sees T = ħg/2πckB.

δQ = T δS on every local horizon  ⇒  Gμν + Λgμν = (8πG/c⁴) Tμν
1/(4Għ) = entanglement per area  ⇒  G = π a² c³ / (N cs ħ),   cs = 0.3046

Jacobson's step is derived symbolically: Raychaudhuri, the null-vector lemma and the contracted Bianchi identity. Its premise, local equilibrium on every local horizon, is assumed. The measured G fixes a²/N: a = 0.31 ℓP for one field and 3.1 ℓP for a hundred. Caveats: cs depends on the regulator, and for gauge fields the area-law coefficient includes contact terms (Kabat 1995), so the identification is cleanest for scalars and fermions.

4 · What measurement really is

Records, not observers

In one line: a quantum outcome becomes definite when the surroundings keep a record of it. Nobody has to look.

Send particles through two slits and you get stripes, an interference pattern. Put a detector at the slits and the stripes fade. The usual story says "observing changes reality". The physics is more down to earth: the stripes fade by exactly as much as the detector recorded which slit was used. The detector doesn't need to be read, switched on to a screen or noticed by anyone. A stray air molecule or photon carrying the information works just as well.

Try it. Slide the detector strength and watch the fringe contrast and the path information trade off. Their squares always add up to exactly 1.

1.00fringe contrast (V)
0.00which-path record (D)

V² + D² = 1.000 at every setting.

Why probabilities are what they are

Quantum chance follows one exact rule: probability = amplitude squared. Here it's derived rather than taken as given: it's the only rule that adds up to 1 and can't be used to send messages faster than light.

The electron isn't split in half

An electron's own electric field is in both branches, but as a possibility, not as half a charge in each. A literally split charge would bind hydrogen at 6.64 eV, against the measured 13.60 eV.

A test you could run in a lab

Fringe loss counts the spread in exchanged energy quanta, so heat destroys more of it. A single trapped ion's contrast should fall from 0.787 to 0.111 as its motion warms from 0.1 to 5 quanta.

Myths vs. physics

What quantum physics does not say

A lot of what circulates as "quantum" is mysticism with physics words attached. Here is what the physics on this page actually says.

Consciousness, or "the observer", creates reality.
Outcomes become definite when the surroundings record them. A photon or an air molecule is enough, and no mind is involved (section 4).
Entanglement lets you send messages instantly.
Entangled correlations appear at a distance, but they can't carry a message. The same rule that sets quantum probabilities forbids it (section 4).
Your thoughts or intentions can change quantum outcomes.
The odds are fixed by amplitude squared, confirmed to many decimal places. No controlled experiment has found intention moving them.
An electron is a tiny ball, or a cloud of charge smeared out in space.
Neither. Its field is in each branch as a possibility. A charge literally spread out would give hydrogen the wrong energy, 6.64 eV instead of 13.60 eV.
"Quantum" healing, energy and wellness products.
Warm, large, wet objects like bodies are constantly recorded by their surroundings, so their quantum superpositions vanish almost instantly. "Quantum" on a label isn't physics.
Gravity and quantum physics can't be talked about together.
At lab scales they fit: gravity is a field, and each branch carries its own (sections 1–2). The genuinely hard problems are at far smaller scales, like black-hole interiors.
Einstein said space is curved, so a "twist" picture must be wrong.
They are two ways of writing the same theory. Einstein explored the twist version himself, and both give identical predictions (section 1).
Physicists just assume the quantum probability rule.
It can be derived. It's the only rule that is self-consistent and doesn't allow faster-than-light messages (section 4).
